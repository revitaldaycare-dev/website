Kindergarten Readiness Checklist: Is Your Child Prepared for Elementary School?

Published: August 23, 2026 · By Revital Edry

Most children enter kindergarten between ages 4 and 5, but readiness isn't about hitting a specific birthday — it's about a constellation of skills that help a child thrive in a structured classroom. If your child is approaching kindergarten age, use this checklist to gauge where they are and where a quality preschool program can help them grow.

Academic Foundations

Recognizes Letters and Sounds — Your child doesn't need to read fluently, but they should recognize most uppercase letters and begin connecting letters to sounds. Singing the alphabet, pointing out letters on signs, and reading together daily all build this foundation.

Counts to 10 and Beyond — Counting objects, recognizing numbers 1–10, and understanding that numbers represent quantity are kindergarten essentials. Most children develop this naturally through play — counting blocks, sorting toys, or helping set the table.

Holds a Pencil and Makes Marks — Fine motor development matters. If your child can grip a crayon, draw basic shapes (circle, square, line), and attempt to write their name, they're on track. Cutting with safety scissors is another positive sign.

Listens to Stories — A child who can sit through a short picture book, recall details, and answer simple questions about the story is building the listening comprehension kindergarten teachers rely on.

Social-Emotional Readiness

Separates from Parents Without Extreme Distress — Brief tears are normal, but a child who can eventually engage in activities after saying goodbye is demonstrating emotional resilience. Preschool is excellent preparation for this transition.

Takes Turns and Shares — Cooperative play emerges around age 3–4. If your child can play alongside peers and is beginning to share toys and take turns, they're developing the social skills kindergarten demands.

Expresses Emotions with Words — Children who can say "I'm sad" or "I'm frustrated" instead of hitting or screaming are building self-regulation — one of the strongest predictors of kindergarten success.

Follows Classroom Routines — Sitting during circle time, lining up, washing hands before eating, and cleaning up after activities — these routines form the backbone of a kindergarten day.

Physical Readiness

Uses the Bathroom Independently — Most kindergartens expect children to manage toileting without assistance, including wiping and washing hands.

Eats Independently — Using a fork and spoon, opening lunch containers, and cleaning up after eating are practical skills that build confidence in the classroom.

Runs, Jumps, and Climbs with Confidence — Gross motor development supports playground socialization and physical education. Children who can navigate climbing structures, kick a ball, and skip are physically ready.

Gets Dressed Without Help — Managing buttons, zippers, and shoes (even Velcro) gives children independence during the school day.

What If Your Child Isn't There Yet?

Every child develops at their own pace. If your child hasn't mastered all these skills, it doesn't mean they're not ready — it means they could benefit from a preschool program that meets them where they are and gently stretches them forward. At Revital Daycare, our 6:1 ratio means we personalize learning for each child's developmental level, building confidence and competence simultaneously.

How Preschool Bridges the Gap — A quality preschool program like Revital's helps children develop academic foundations (letter recognition, counting, pre-writing), social skills (sharing, conflict resolution, classroom routines), and self-confidence (separating from parents, expressing needs, trying new things) in a safe, nurturing environment.

The best kindergarten readiness predictor isn't flashcard drilling — it's a child who feels confident, curious, and capable. That's what we build every day at Revital.

Reggio Emilia vs Montessori: Which Preschool Approach Is Right for Your Child?

Published: August 22, 2026 · By Revital Edry

When choosing a preschool in Winnetka, you'll quickly notice that most programs describe themselves as either "Montessori" or "Reggio Emilia-inspired." Both are respected, research-backed approaches — but they work differently, and one may suit your child better than the other. Here's an honest comparison to help you decide.

What Is Montessori?

Developed by Dr. Maria Montessori in early 1900s Italy, the Montessori method emphasizes self-directed activity, hands-on learning, and collaborative play. Children choose from a curated set of materials on shelves, working independently or in small groups while teachers guide from the side. The classroom is highly structured with specific materials designed to teach one concept at a time.

What Is Reggio Emilia?

Created by educator Loris Malaguzzi after World War II, the Reggio Emilia approach treats children as capable, curious protagonists in their own learning. Rather than following preset materials, teachers observe children's interests and build projects around them. The classroom itself is the "third teacher" — filled with natural light, plants, mirrors, and open-ended materials that invite exploration.

Key Differences

Structure — Montessori uses a structured sequence of materials; children follow a progression. Reggio Emilia is more emergent — curriculum evolves based on what children are curious about. If your child thrives on routine and order, Montessori may feel more comfortable. If they're naturally exploratory and creative, Reggio may be a better fit.

Teacher Role — In Montessori, the teacher is a "guide" who observes and introduces materials when ready. In Reggio Emilia, the teacher is a "co-learner" who follows the child's lead and designs experiences in real time. Both approaches value the teacher's expertise, but the relationship with the child looks different.

Learning Materials — Montessori uses specific, purpose-built materials (the pink tower, cylinder blocks, sandpaper letters). Reggio Emilia uses open-ended, often natural materials — clay, wood, fabric, found objects — that can be used in unlimited ways.

Documentation — Reggio Emilia places heavy emphasis on documenting children's work through photos, transcriptions, and displays. This visible record helps children reflect on their learning and gives parents deep insight into classroom life. Montessori tracks progress through the child's mastery of specific materials.

Group Work — Montessori often emphasizes individual or small-group work. Reggio Emilia frequently engages entire groups in long-term projects that can last weeks, building collaboration and communication skills.

Which Approach Fits Your Family?

Consider Montessori if: Your child likes structure and order, learns well independently, or is drawn to specific, hands-on materials with clear outcomes.

Consider Reggio Emilia if: Your child is highly curious and creative, enjoys collaborative projects, expresses themselves through art and storytelling, or thrives in a warm, home-like environment.

Our curriculum honors children's curiosity while ensuring they develop the literacy, math, and social skills they need for elementary school.

Why Reggio Emilia Works: What Winnetka Parents Should Know About This Teaching Philosophy

Published: August 18, 2026 · By Revital Edry

You may have heard the term "Reggio Emilia" when researching preschools, but what does it actually mean — and why do so many parents in Winnetka seek out programs that use this approach?

What Is Reggio Emilia? — Developed in post-WWII Italy by educator Loris Malaguzzi, the Reggio Emilia approach treats children as capable, curious learners. Rather than following a rigid, teacher-directed curriculum, it emphasizes child-led exploration guided by a supportive teacher. The classroom itself is considered the "third teacher" — intentionally designed to inspire discovery.

Core Principles That Matter for Your Child

Child-Led Learning — Teachers observe each child's interests and build learning experiences around them. If a child is fascinated by dinosaurs, that curiosity becomes a vehicle for math (counting, measuring), science (fossils, habitats), literacy (reading about dinosaurs), and art (drawing, sculpting).

Project-Based Exploration — Rather than isolated worksheets, children engage in extended projects that deepen understanding over weeks. This builds critical thinking, persistence, and the ability to see connections between ideas.

The Hundred Languages of Children — Malaguzzi's famous concept recognizes that children express understanding in many ways: through drawing, building, dramatic play, music, and conversation. A Reggio-inspired classroom honors all of these "languages."

Documentation as Learning — Teachers photograph, record, and display children's work, creating a visible record of learning. This helps children reflect on their growth and helps parents see the depth of what happens in the classroom.

How Revital Daycare Uses Reggio Principles — Our curriculum draws on Reggio Emilia philosophy while incorporating structured learning objectives for kindergarten readiness. Teachers design activities based on children's emerging interests, and our classroom layout invites exploration across art, science, literacy, and sensory areas.

What to Expect on a Preschool Tour: A Winnetka Parent's Checklist

Published: August 10, 2026 · By Revital Edry

A preschool tour is your chance to see beyond the website and really evaluate whether a program is right for your child. Here's a practical checklist for Winnetka parents touring any local preschool — including Revital Daycare.

Before You Go

Check Licensing Status — Look up the facility on California's CDSS Community Care Licensing website. Confirm the license is active and review any inspection findings. Revital Daycare's CDSS License #197312451 is fully active.

Prepare Questions — Write down what matters most to you. Common high-value questions include: What is your staff-to-child ratio? How do you handle discipline? What curriculum do you follow? What are your teacher qualifications? How do you communicate with parents?

During the Tour

Observe Teacher-Child Interactions — Are teachers at eye level with children? Do they speak respectfully and warmly? Are they engaged or distracted? At Revital, our teachers form genuine bonds with every child — our 6:1 ratio makes this possible.

Evaluate the Physical Space — Look for clean, well-maintained facilities with age-appropriate furniture, organized learning centers, and adequate outdoor space. Check for safety features: secured entry, covered outdoor areas, and childproofed hazards.

Ask About Daily Routine — A structured but flexible schedule signals good classroom management. Ask how the day balances learning, play, meals, and rest. Request a sample schedule to review at home.

Inquire About Communication — How will you know what your child did each day? Do teachers send photos, daily reports, or app updates? Regular parent-teacher communication is essential.

After the Tour

Trust Your Instincts — Did the environment feel welcoming? Did the teachers seem genuinely passionate? Did your child seem comfortable? Your gut feeling matters as much as any checklist.

Compare Notes — If you're touring multiple preschools, create a simple comparison chart across the criteria that matter most to you.

5 Signs Your Child Is Ready for Preschool

Published: March 15, 2026 · Last Updated: August 2026 · By Revital Edry

Wondering if your little one is ready for preschool? Here are five key developmental signs that indicate your child is prepared to thrive in a structured learning environment.

1. Follows Simple Instructions — Children who can follow two-step directions like "pick up your toy and put it in the bin" are ready for classroom routines.

2. Shows Interest in Other Children — If your child enjoys playing alongside or with peers, they'll benefit from the social environment of preschool.

4. Demonstrates Independence — Small acts like putting on shoes, feeding themselves, or cleaning up after play show readiness for structured activities.

5. Shows Curiosity About Learning — Children who ask questions, explore their environment, and show interest in books and art are primed for our curriculum.

Preparing Your Child for Their First Day of Preschool

Published: January 20, 2026 · Last Updated: August 2026 · By Revital Edry

The first day of preschool is a milestone for both children and parents. Here are our tried-and-true tips to make the transition smooth and positive.

Visit the School Together — Schedule a tour before the first day so your child can explore the classroom, meet teachers, and feel comfortable in the new environment.

Establish a Routine — A few weeks before school starts, practice the morning routine: wake up, get dressed, eat breakfast, and leave at the same time each day.

Read Books About Preschool — Stories like "The Kissing Hand" or "Llama Llama Misses Mama" help children understand and process their feelings about starting school.

Create a Goodbye Ritual — A quick, consistent goodbye (like a special handshake or kiss on the palm) gives children a sense of security and predictability.

Talk Positively About School — Share excitement about the fun activities, new friends, and caring teachers they'll meet. Avoid expressing your own anxieties in front of your child.

The Benefits of Play-Based Learning in Early Childhood

Published: December 10, 2025 · Last Updated: August 2026 · By Revital Edry

Play isn't just fun — it's how young children learn best. Research consistently shows that play-based learning develops critical cognitive, social, and emotional skills that traditional drilling methods simply cannot match.

Cognitive Development — Through play, children explore cause and effect, develop problem-solving skills, and build mathematical concepts naturally. Building with blocks teaches geometry; playing store teaches counting.

Language Growth — Pretend play scenarios expand vocabulary and communication skills. When children negotiate roles in a game or narrate their play, they're developing sophisticated language abilities.

Social Skills — Cooperative play teaches sharing, turn-taking, conflict resolution, and empathy — skills that form the foundation for healthy relationships throughout life.

Emotional Regulation — Play provides a safe space for children to express emotions, practice self-control, and build resilience when things don't go as planned.
